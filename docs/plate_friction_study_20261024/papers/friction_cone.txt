Keeping Contact: Task-Adaptive Friction Margins
for Bimanual Non-Prehensile Manipulation
Anonymous authors
Abstract—Maintaining contact in non-prehensile manipulation
requires more than nominal friction cone feasibility: a force
barely inside the cone and one well inside it are both feasible, but
they provide different margins against slip and contact loss. This
paper uses signed friction distance to measure friction margin
in force space and derives a task-adaptive margin from the
force correction needed to balance pivot moment deviations in
bimanual tilting. The resulting contact margins are embedded in
a sequence of second-order cone programs (SOCPs), each giving
a globally optimal contact force solution at a tilt angle. The
optimizer balances the object about an environmental pivot while
keeping robot and pivot contact forces away from the nominal
friction boundary. Optimizer-level results show interpretable
force redistribution and adaptation to object property changes.
Real-world experiments demonstrate successful execution under
object property variations and model mismatch, while fixed
margin and no-margin comparisons illustrate the importance of
adapting the margin to task demand. These results support signed
friction distance margins as a force planning mechanism for
maintaining contact in bimanual non-prehensile manipulation.
Index Terms—Manipulation planning, bimanual manipulation,
contact force planning, friction margin, convex optimization.
I. INTRODUCTION
Non-prehensile manipulation extends robotic manipulation
beyond rigid grasping by exploiting contact interactions as part
of the task mechanics. This enables behaviors such as pushing
[1], rolling [2], and pivoting [3]. Since the resulting motion is
governed by frictional contacts, planning must reason about
both contact motion and forces. Recent optimization-based
formulations incorporate trajectory optimization with frictional
contact constraints and simultaneous contact selection [4, 5].
In these formulations, friction constraints primarily specify
admissible contact forces or contact modes along the motion.
Yet admissibility does not specify where a maintained contact
force lies within the friction cone: a nominally feasible force
profile may still operate near a friction boundary and remain
sensitive to modeling error or tracking deviation.
A useful clue comes from everyday manipulation. When
lifting an unfamiliar object, a person rarely knows its inertial
parameters exactly, yet contact is often maintained stably.
Rather than regulating contact force at the minimum value
that barely avoids slip, people maintain a margin above the slip
threshold and adjust it as perceived task demand changes [6,
7]. This contrasts with friction cones used only as feasibility
tests: a force that barely clears the boundary and one well
inside the cone are accepted alike, although only the latter
carries a meaningful margin. The question is thus not merely
whether a planned force is feasible, but what margin it should
maintain and how that margin should vary with the task.
Fig. 1: Bimanual non-prehensile tilting. Inset illustrates two feasible contact

Motivated by this view, we plan contact forces by lower
bounding their signed distance from the nominal friction
boundary. This lower bound gives each maintained contact a
force space margin against deviations from the planned force.
The remaining question is how to choose this margin along
the motion: a fixed value treats margin as uniform, while a
task-adaptive value can vary with the force demand imposed
by the manipulation task. We study this construction on the
planar bimanual tilting task in Fig. 1. This task has a structured
but nonuniform force requirement: the gravitational moment
varies with tilt angle and object properties, changing the forces
required from the two robot contacts. The motion is especially
sensitive to slip at the initial and final configurations, making
sufficient tangential support and friction margin important
throughout the tilt. Unlike prior pivoting studies that often
rely on symmetric robot–object contact arrangements [3, 8],
our setup uses non-opposing contacts and exploits an environ-
mental pivot to accomplish the task. Maintaining both robot
contacts is therefore central to the task, since slip or contact
loss at either contact can lead to task failure.
In summary, the main contributions of this work are:
1) We use signed friction distance to the nominal friction
boundary as an explicit force space measure of margin for
contact force planning.
2) We introduce a task-adaptive margin construction that
lower bounds this signed distance based on the force
demand required by the task.
3) We embed this margin in a convex force planner with
globally optimal contact force solutions and evaluate it in
real-world experiments.

II. RELATEDWORK
A. Non-Prehensile Manipulation with Force Planning
Prior work in non-prehensile manipulation has shown that
useful object motion can be generated by exploiting frictional
and environmental contacts instead of rigidly grasping the
object [9–11]. Existing methods have studied contact mode
planning, reorientation with environmental support, and bi-
manual coordination under changing contact conditions [3,
8, 12–15]. These works demonstrate the value of planning
through contact, but many emphasize object motion, contact
modes, or contact feasibility. For non-prehensile manipulation,
force planning is also central because the object is not rigidly
held. Even with a prescribed motion and contact mode, the
contact force distribution determines whether the required
wrench can be transmitted without slip or unloading [9,
16]. However, less attention has been given to how large a
friction margin should be maintained at each contact during
execution, especially when the contacts are non-opposing and
the required wrench changes over the motion. Bimanual tilting
with an environmental pivot exposes this issue: losing either
robot contact can remove the force needed to support or
regulate the object.
B. Contact Force Optimization and Friction Margins
Optimization is widely used in contact-rich manipulation
to reason about object motion, contact forces, and frictional
constraints [4, 5, 17]. For known contact geometry and motion,
force planning becomes more structured and can be formulated
as a convex optimization problem, such as a quadratic program
or second-order cone program [18–20]. Beyond computing
feasible contact forces, an important question is how explicitly
the optimized forces maintain margin from frictional limits.
Friction margins have been used in broader manipulation
optimization problems to improve feasibility and stability [21,
22]. Such margins are useful, but they are not generally
selected as local contact-wise force space margins. At the
local contact level, conservatism can be introduced through the
friction model or objective, such as using a reduced friction
coefficient or adding penalty and barrier terms [23]. Cone
shrinking provides a relative margin that vanishes as normal
force approaches zero, while objective penalties depend on
weights and compete with the force objective. Recent work
has used a minimum friction margin to generate behavior that
is distilled into a learning-based controller [24]. There, the
margin serves as an intermediate data generation constraint
rather than a constraint directly enforced during contact force
planning, and its required value is fixed. When task force de-
mand changes over a motion, a fixed margin can be insufficient
in demanding configurations or add unnecessary loading when
demand is small.
Together, these works leave open how to impose a contact-
wise margin directly on planned contact forces, with a force
space interpretation, while allowing the required margin to
vary with task demand. We address this by enforcing a
task-adaptive lower bound on the signed distance from each
planned contact force to the nominal friction boundary as a
hard constraint in a convex force planner.

III. METHOD

measure of force space margin. We then derive the pivot-
centered task demand and the resulting affine task-adaptive
margin for bimanual tilting, and embeds the margin in a
convex force planner. Unless otherwise stated, vectors are
expressed in the object frame and norms are Euclidean.

IV. EXPERIMENTS
A. Setup
As illustrated in Fig. 1, the experiments used two 7-DoF
Kinova Gen3 manipulators for bimanual tilting. Each wrist
had an ATI Mini40 force/torque sensor and a hemispherical
end-effector covered with friction tape, as shown in Fig. 4a.
The manipulated box measured0.3×0.2×0.2 mand had a
base mass of1.7 kg. The nominal condition added a centered
200 gcalibration weight using the plate in Fig. 4b, giving a
total mass of1.9 kgand defining the centered CoM model. The
measured friction coefficients, averaged over three trials, were
µ1 =µ 2 = 0.75for the robot–object contacts andµ p = 0.5for
the object–environment contact. The margin valuesλ m = 0.25
andλ CoM = 0.5 Nare design parameters selected once from
the nominal setup and kept fixed across all conditions. Thus,
the experiments evaluate the resulting contact behavior rather
than identifying tight physical deviation bounds. For the fixed
experimental contact geometry, the resulting margins were
ρ1(θ)≈0.56ρ(θ),ρ 2(θ)≈0.83ρ(θ), andρ p(θ)≈1.39ρ(θ).
The optimization was implemented in CVXPY [26] and solved
with ECOS [27] at0.1 ◦ angle increments. Execution used
impedance control with translational stiffness1000 N/mand
rotational stiffness20 Nm/rad.

V. RESULTS
In this section, we evaluate the proposed method from five
perspectives:
1) whether the optimized contact forces follow the task me-
chanics under nominal tilting,
2) whether the task-adaptive margin captures object property
effects through the task-demand model,
3) whether the method remains effective under model mis-
match,
4) whether task-adaptive margins improve robustness over a
fixed margin,
5) whether positive margins support successful task execution.
A. Contact Force Profiles and Margin Effects
We first examine how the optimized forces follow the task
mechanics in the nominal condition and how the margin
changes their distance from the friction boundary. Figure 5
compares optimized force profiles with and without the task-
adaptive margin, together with experimental force profiles.
For the box with centered CoM, the geometric tipping line
occurs at approximatelyθ tip = 55 ◦, where the gravity line
of action nearly aligns with the environmental pivot. The
gravitational momentτ g(θ)about the pivot and the optimized
force demand therefore decrease nearθ tip and increase after
the object passes this configuration. The optimized tangential
forcesf t also transition from support to braking. Under the
contact force sign convention in Fig. 3, the tangential forces
are positive beforeθ tip, when the robot contacts oppose gravity
to support rotation, and negative afterward, when gravity
assists the rotation. The experimental responses follow the
optimized profiles and show the same transition, although
a magnitude offset remains, consistent with pose deviations
under impedance execution and unmodeled contact effects.
Figure 5 also shows how the margins affect the optimized
force distribution. Both margins decrease nearθ tip as task
force demand falls, while the right contact margin remains
larger because that contact has greater moment leverage about
the pivot, as reflected in Eq. (25). Near tipping, the normal
forces remain above the no margin solution due to the force-
independent term, preventing the optimized normal forces
from collapsing toward zero. The force plane insets show these
effects geometrically: the margin moves the optimized force
away from the nominal friction boundary at early tilt, and
retains a positive normal force near tipping. The tangential
forces also change to satisfy the same moment balance with
the full contact force vectors.
We also measured solve time at each tilt angle. The no mar-
gin case setsρ i(θ) = 0, while the with margin case changes
only the lower bound in the same SOC friction constraint.
Both preserve the same SOCP structure. Mean solve times
were8.99±1.22ms with the margin and9.05±1.45ms
without it, indicating comparable computational costs.
B. Adaptation to Object Property Variations
We next examine how the margin adapts to object property
changes through the task-demand model and how this adap-
tation affects the optimized force profiles. Figure 7 shows the
summed marginρ Σ =ρ 1 +ρ 2 and combined force magnitude
FΣ =∥f 1∥+∥f 2∥for the variations listed in Table I.
In the mass sweep, changing mass scales the gravitational
moment over the motion without changing the tipping angle.
Increasing mass produces largerρ Σ and optimizedF Σ, while
the lighter object produces lower profiles. This illustrates
the role of the task-dependent gain: scaling the margin with
Ftask(θ)places a larger margin at high demand while reducing
the optimized loading near tipping configuration.
In the CoM sweep, shifting the CoM changes the moment
arm inτ g(θ). The resulting moment differences are larger at
early tilt angles, where the gravity lines of action have more
distinct perpendicular distances to the pivot. These distances
converge toward the final configuration, bringing the moment
demands, margins, and force profiles closer together. The CoM
shift also changes where the gravity line passes through the
pivot, shifting the profile minima. This sensitivity motivates
the force-independent margin term: CoM deviations can in-
duce nonzero force corrections even when at low nominal task
demand, thus this term prevents the margin from collapsing in
these configurations.
Table II summarizes the execution outcomes. All trials under
object property variations completed with contact maintained.
These results support the adaptability of the margin law:
using the same parameters, the margin followsF task(θ)under
mass and CoM variations, producing the expected scaling
and shifting trends while maintaining contact across all tested
conditions.

VI. CONCLUSION
This work presented signed friction distance margins for
contact force planning in bimanual non-prehensile manip-
ulation. Lower bounding each planned force’s distance to
the nominal friction boundary yields a force space margin
directly comparable to force deviations. For bimanual tilting,
pivot moment deviations yield an affine task-adaptive margin,
allocated to robot contacts by their moment contributions
and combined with a heuristic pivot margin in a convex
planner. The optimized and measured force profiles reflected
the evaluated object property variations, and all task-adaptive
trials completed successfully under all tested task-adaptive
conditions. At matched average combined force magnitude,
the fixed margin completed the nominal trials but failed under
model mismatch, supporting configuration dependent margin
allocation to accommodate model uncertainty. The no-margin
ablation produced a larger violation fraction that led to contact
loss and task failure.
The same margin principle can be generalized by defining
the task-level margin from the largest minimum-norm con-
tact force correction over admissible wrench deviations, with

support lower bounding signed friction distance as a physically
interpretable means of moving contact force planning beyond
nominal friction feasibility toward force space margins.
